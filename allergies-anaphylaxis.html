    Step 2

    The immune system makes a mistake

    Read first

    Allergies are a chronic disease that occurs when the immune system overreacts to a substance in the environment. The substance itself — a peanut, a bee sting, a dose of penicillin — is harmless to most people. In someone with an allergy, the immune system treats it as an invader and attacks.

    That attack is what you see and feel. Symptoms include sneezing, runny nose, itchy eyes, rash, swelling, and trouble breathing.

    Where allergens come from

    • Food — peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, shellfish, wheat
    • Inhalants — pollen, mold, animal dander, dust
    • Insect stings — bee and wasp
    • Medications — penicillin, aspirin

    Because allergies are chronic, a person does not outgrow the risk on a given day the way you shake off a cold. The trigger has to be avoided, and a plan has to be ready.

    Step 3

    Nine foods, ninety percent of reactions

    Read first

    The U.S. Food and Drug Administration identifies nine major food allergens. Together they account for 90% of all allergic reactions in the United States, which is why they are the ones listed on package labels.

    The nine are milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame seeds. Reading a label for those nine words is the single most useful habit for someone managing a food allergy — or for someone bringing food to a group.

    Step 4

    “I’m kind of allergic to dairy”

    Read first

    People use the word allergy loosely, and the difference matters in an emergency. These are two different body systems doing two different things.

    Food intolerance

    Happens in the digestive system, not the immune system. The body has difficulty digesting or metabolizing a specific food. Some people can tolerate eating small amounts of it.

    Food allergy

    An immune system attack. It can be life-threatening, and there is no safe small amount. Zero tolerance.

    So a classmate who says dairy “messes with my stomach” and a classmate whose throat closes from dairy are describing two different situations. The second one is the reason you learn the rest of this lesson.

    Step 5

    Mild, moderate, severe

    Read first

    An allergic reaction is the response the body has to an allergen: the immune system overreacts, causing mild, moderate, or severe symptoms. Reactions sit on a spectrum, and a reaction can move up that spectrum quickly.

    Mild — usually skin symptoms, such as hives or itching.
    Moderate — may include respiratory symptoms, such as wheezing or difficulty breathing.
    Severe (anaphylaxis) — a life-threatening reaction that can cause widespread swelling, difficulty breathing, and a drop in blood pressure.

    Notice what separates the levels: not how loudly someone complains, but which body systems are involved. Skin alone is mild. Once breathing is involved, the reaction has escalated.

    Step 6

    Two or more systems

    Read first

    Anaphylaxis is distinguished from a mild or moderate allergic reaction by the sudden involvement of two or more organ systems, showing up as symptoms such as difficulty breathing, swelling of the tongue, swelling or tightness in the throat, wheezing, sudden persistent cough, abdominal pain, vomiting, and hypotension (low blood pressure).

    The onset of anaphylaxis can occur within seconds of exposure to the allergen, or it can be delayed up to an hour or more. A person who seems fine ten minutes after eating is not necessarily out of the woods.

    Sorting symptoms by system

    • Skin and mucosal — hives, itching, swelling of the tongue, swelling or tightness in the throat
    • Respiratory — wheezing, sudden persistent cough, difficulty breathing
    • Digestive — abdominal pain, vomiting
    • Cardiovascular — hypotension, a drop in blood pressure

    Worked example: a person eats a food they are allergic to and develops wheezing (respiratory), tongue swelling (mucosal), and hives (skin). Two or more body systems are involved, so the verdict is severe — anaphylaxis — and immediate intervention is required.

    Step 7

    Epinephrine and 911

    Read first

    The treatment for anaphylaxis is epinephrine by injection and a call to 911. Both, every time.

    Epinephrine is the only medication that can reverse the symptoms of anaphylaxis. Administering it without delay is the key to saving a life — waiting can be fatal. If symptoms are not better in 5 minutes, repeat the injection. After that comes observation in the emergency room for 4 to 6 hours, because symptoms can return.

    There is no version of this where you wait and see.An antihistamine is not a substitute. Feeling embarrassed about a false alarm is not a reason to delay.

    Blue to the sky, orange to the thigh

    The four steps

    • Blue end up — pull off the blue safety cap
    • Orange end down, pushed firmly against the outer thigh
    • Push and hold in place, count to 10 (we count fast in emergencies)
    • Call 911

    The phrase to keep in your head is blue to the sky, orange to the thigh. The blue cap comes off the top; the orange end is the end that delivers the medication, and it goes into the outer thigh. Getting those ends backwards is the mistake people make when they panic.

    Students who self-carry epinephrine often carry the Auvi-Q brand. It is a compact, flat device that fits in a pocket, and it talks you through the injection out loud once you pull it from its case. Same medication, same urgency, different shape.

    You may be the person standing next to someone holding one of these. Knowing that both devices exist — and that the Auvi-Q will give you voice instructions — means you will not lose seconds trying to figure out what you are holding.

    Step 10 · Case files

    Maya and Liam

    Read first

    Two students, two reactions, two completely different responses. Before you work the cases, here is what you need that the earlier steps did not cover.

    When the allergen is in the air

    Pollen from ragweed and grasses is an inhalant allergen. When it gets in, the immune system releases chemicals like histamine, and those chemicals cause local inflammation — sneezing, watery red eyes, a stuffy nose. The reaction stays in one area of the body, so it is mild, and it eases up once the person is away from the pollen.

    Mild inhalant allergies are managed with an over-the-counter antihistamine, plus habits that cut down exposure: changing clothes and showering right after an outdoor practice washes the pollen off instead of carrying it around all evening.

    Why 911 is not optional

    The effects of epinephrine are temporary — roughly 15 to 20 minutes — and symptoms can come back after the person seems fine. That return is called a biphasic reaction, and it is why professional emergency care is required even when someone improves right after the injection.

    Prevention for a known food allergy is ordinary and specific: read food labels carefully, tell whoever is preparing the food about the allergy, avoid unlabeled home-baked goods, and carry an EpiPen at all times.

    Case 1 — Maya

    Maya, a 15-year-old high school student, loves playing outdoor soccer in the fall. During outdoor practices she has sneezing fits, watery red eyes, and a stuffy nose. She notices the symptoms go away when she is indoors.

    Case 2 — Liam

    Liam, 16, attends a party and eats a bakery brownie. Five minutes later he feels his throat tightening, his lips swell, hives cover his skin, and he feels dizzy and short of breath.

    Step 11 · Case files

    Jordan and Sam

    Read first

    Not every allergen is eaten or inhaled. Two more kinds show up often in high school, and neither one behaves the way a food allergy does.

    Allergens you touch

    A contact allergy is a delayed hypersensitivity. Nickel — in belt buckles, snaps, jewelry, and watch backs — is one of the most common causes. Immune cells in the skin take 24 to 48 hours to react, so the rash shows up a day or two after the contact rather than within minutes, and it appears only where the material actually touched the skin.

    Treatment is simple: remove the item, wash the skin with mild soap, and apply hydrocortisone or a soothing lotion. Prevention means avoiding nickel, choosing hypoallergenic materials, or covering the back of a metal buckle with tape or cloth.

    Reactions to medication

    A rash from a medication, with no trouble breathing, no facial swelling, and no dizziness, is a mild to moderate reaction rather than a life-threatening one. It still matters. Nobody should stop a prescribed antibiotic or start another drug on their own — the prescribing doctor or pharmacist decides that.

    The reaction also belongs in the person's medical file, so that every future provider knows to avoid that family of antibiotics and a worse reaction never gets a chance to happen.

    Case 3 — Jordan

    Jordan, 15, bought a new belt with a metallic buckle. After wearing it for two days he developed a red, itchy, bumpy rash right around his waist, where the buckle pressed against his skin.

    Case 4 — Sam

    Sam, 16, is prescribed amoxicillin, an antibiotic, for an illness. On day four of taking the pills Sam develops a red, blotchy rash across their chest. Sam has no trouble breathing and no swelling.
