    Our working definition

    A drug is any substance, other than food, that changes how the body or mind works.

    That covers medicines and caffeine as well as illegal drugs. Legal doesn’t mean harmless, and prescribed doesn’t mean safe for everyone.

    Race to the brain

    How a drug gets into the blood

    A drug can’t affect the brain until it gets into the blood. Blood from the lungs goes to the heart, which pumps it straight out to the brain, so a drug that enters the blood there arrives within seconds. The way a drug enters the body is called its route of administration, and three things decide how fast a route works.

    How direct the path isA needle puts a drug right into a vein. The lungs have very thin walls and a huge surface area, about the size of half a tennis court, so drugs cross into the blood almost instantly.
    How thin the lining isThe inside of the nose and the area under the tongue have thin, moist linings packed with tiny blood vessels. Drugs soak through them quickly.
    What’s in the wayA swallowed drug has to get through the stomach and intestines, then pass through the liver, which breaks some of it down first. Skin is built to keep things out, so drugs cross it very slowly.

        A fast route makes a quick, intense spike of reward, then a sharp drop as the drug wears off. The brain strongly links the drug with that spike, and the crash creates a pull to take another dose sooner. That cycle makes repeated use, and then dependence, more likely.

        Why the brain keeps score

        Your brain has a reward circuit. When something good happens, like a favorite song, a win, or a meal with friends, neurons release dopamine. It’s a signal that says “that mattered, do it again.” It’s how you learn what’s worth repeating.

        Many drugs hijack that circuit. They can cause a bigger and faster dopamine surge than natural rewards. The National Institute on Drug Abuse describes it as roughly 2 to 10 times as much.

        Why teens are more vulnerable: the reward system matures earlier than the prefrontal cortex, the part that plans and weighs consequences, which keeps developing into the mid-20s. A teen brain gets strong “go” signals with weaker brakes. Starting use earlier raises the chance of developing a disorder.
