    Blood alcohol concentration

    BAC is the percent of alcohol (ethyl alcohol, or ethanol) in a person’s bloodstream.

    A BAC of 0.10% means a person’s blood contains one part alcohol for every 1,000 parts blood. That sounds tiny, but alcohol is a depressant: it slows the brain and nervous system, so even small amounts change how a person thinks, moves, and reacts.

      What affects BAC

      Ten factors go into someone’s BAC. Four of them matter most.

      1. AmountHow much alcohol is consumed. This is the single biggest factor.
      2. SpeedHow fast it’s consumed. Drinking quickly means the liver can’t keep up.
      3. Biological sexFemales typically have a higher body-fat percentage and less body water, so the same drink produces a higher BAC.
      4. Body sizeSmaller bodies have less blood volume and body water to dilute alcohol.

      Other factors

      1. Age. Older adults have lower blood volume.
      2. Food in the stomach. Food delays absorption. It does not prevent it.
      3. Feelings. Stress, anger, and fear can change how alcohol is absorbed and how it affects you.
      4. Medications (prescription or over-the-counter). Many interact dangerously with alcohol.
      5. Carbonated beverages. Bubbles move alcohol into the bloodstream faster.
      6. Hormone levels. Hormones affect how quickly the body processes alcohol, which is part of why males and females differ.

      Tolerance is NOT a factor in BAC. Tolerance means a person needs more alcohol to feel the same effect. It changes how drunk someone feels, but the amount of alcohol in their blood is the same. Someone with high tolerance can have a dangerous BAC and not realize it.

      Cirrhosis symptoms can include weight loss, nausea and vomiting, easy bruising, bleeding gums, yellowing of the skin and eyes, swelling of the abdomen, and loss of appetite.

      Effects on other organs and body functions

      • Alcohol decreases body temperature, even though the drinker feels warmer.
      • Alcohol increases urine output, which causes dehydration.
      • Alcohol increases gastric juices, which can cause or irritate an ulcer.

      Binge drinking

      A binge is a pattern of drinking that brings BAC to 0.08 or above. For a typical adult, that’s about 5 or more drinks for males or 4 or more drinks for females in about 2 hours.

      Blackout vs. pass out

      Blackout

      A period of time a person cannot remember. They may still be awake, walking, and talking, but their brain isn’t recording memories.

      Pass out

      The person loses consciousness and cannot be woken up. Roll them onto their side and call 911 immediately.

      Know how to respond to an emergency

      Alcohol poisoning happens when someone drinks so much that alcohol begins to shut down basic life functions like breathing, heart rate, and temperature control.

      1. Recognize the signs of alcohol poisoning
      2. Call 911
      3. Place them in the recovery position

      12. At a party, you find a friend on the bathroom floor. They threw up, their skin is cold and clammy, and you can’t wake them. What should you do first?

      Step 2: Call 911

      Sometimes teens are afraid to call 911, especially if they’ve been drinking themselves. Many states, including Illinois, have Good Samaritan (medical amnesty) laws for this exact situation.

      Illinois law for minors. Since January 1, 2016, a person under 21 who calls 911 for someone who needs medical help because of alcohol, and the person who needs help, can be protected from underage drinking charges. To be protected, the caller must give their full name, stay at the scene until help arrives, and cooperate with police and medical staff. It does not protect everyone at the party.

      Read the law (235 ILCS 5/6-20)

      Not getting help has consequences too. A person can face criminal charges, including involuntary manslaughter, if their failure to get help leads to someone’s death. You’ll see this in Tim Piazza’s story.

      Reducing alcohol-related harm

      The most effective strategies for teens are not drinking and avoiding places where underage drinking is happening.

      If you or others are going to be around alcohol:
      • Don’t drink and drive, and don’t get in a car with a driver who has been drinking. Call a sober friend, a parent, or a rideshare.
      • Don’t do risky activities like swimming, playing with fire, sexual activity, or operating vehicles or machinery.
      • Have a designated sober person who watches out for everyone. You can volunteer.
      • Use the buddy system and have a safe ride home planned before you go.

      Four harm reduction strategies

      1. Best not to use any depressants. If using, cut back.
      2. Watch dose and dosage: know what a standard drink is.
      3. Don’t mix substances. Combining depressants, like “benzos” and alcohol, drastically raises the risk of overdose and death.
      4. Know how to respond to an emergency.

      Tim Piazza’s story

      A real-life binge drinking tragedy, and why it matters to you.

      Tim Piazza was a 19-year-old sophomore engineering student at Penn State. In February 2017, on the night he pledged the Beta Theta Pi fraternity, he was pressured to drink a dangerous amount of alcohol in a short time as part of a hazing ritual. He fell down a flight of basement stairs and was badly hurt. Fraternity members saw him throughout the night, but no one called 911 until the next morning, nearly 12 hours after his fall. Tim died two days later.

      The legal consequences

      The Piazza case became one of the largest hazing prosecutions in U.S. history. On May 5, 2017, after a grand jury investigation, 18 fraternity members were charged in connection with Tim’s death. Eight were charged with involuntary manslaughter, and the rest with other offenses, including hazing.

      The fraternity itself was also charged, and Penn State banned its chapter from campus indefinitely. As of September 2017, the fraternity and its 18 members faced a combined total of more than 850 criminal charges.

      In 2018, Pennsylvania passed the Timothy J. Piazza Antihazing Law, which made serious hazing a felony.
