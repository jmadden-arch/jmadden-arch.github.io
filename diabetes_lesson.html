02

The fuel system, working normally

Before you can understand what goes wrong, you need to know what right looks like.

Read first

Every cell in your body runs on glucose, a simple sugar. You get it from food, mostly from carbohydrates like bread, rice, pasta, fruit, and milk. Your digestive system breaks those down into glucose and releases it into your bloodstream, where it travels to cells that need fuel. Glucose is the main energy source for your whole body and the primary fuel for your brain.

But glucose cannot simply walk into a cell. Cell doors stay locked, and the key is a hormone called insulin, made by the pancreas — a small organ tucked behind your stomach. After you eat, glucose levels in your blood rise, the pancreas notices and releases insulin, insulin attaches to your cells, the doors open, and glucose moves inside to be burned for energy.

That handoff takes five steps, and every one of them has to work. Diabetes is what happens when one of them fails.

    Read next

    Two ways the system breaks

    Not enough insulin. The pancreas doesn't make enough insulin to match the glucose entering the blood, so there aren't enough keys for the locks.

    Insulin resistance. There are plenty of keys, but the cell's locks stop responding to them.

    Either way the result is the same: the cell doors stay shut, glucose backs up in the bloodstream, and blood sugar climbs. That is hyperglycemia, or high blood sugar. Here is the strange part — the cells are starving while sitting in a bloodstream full of fuel they cannot use.

    Some numbers for reference. In someone without diabetes, blood glucose usually runs between about 72 and 99 mg/dL before a meal. A diagnosis of diabetes often involves readings around 200 mg/dL or higher. The A1C test, a blood test that reflects average glucose over roughly three months, indicates diabetes at 6.5% or above.

    04

    Type 1 and Type 2 are different diseases

    They share a name and a symptom list, but they start in completely different places. This is the section to slow down on.

    Read first

    Both types end up in the same place — too much glucose in the blood — but they get there by different routes, which is why they are treated differently and why blaming someone for either one misses the point.

    Type 1

    An autoimmune condition

    What happens: the immune system, which normally fights viruses and bacteria, mistakenly attacks and destroys the cells in the pancreas that make insulin.

    Result: the body produces little or no insulin at all. There are almost no keys.

    Who gets it: it can appear at any age, though it often shows up in childhood or the teen years. It is not caused by weight, diet, or anything the person did. Having a parent or sibling with it raises your chances.

    Management: everyone with Type 1 needs insulin every day to stay alive, through injections, a pen, or a wearable pump, balanced against food and activity.

    Type 2

    Insulin resistance

    What happens: the pancreas still makes insulin, but cells stop responding to it properly. The pancreas compensates by working harder, and over time it may not keep up.

    Result: there are plenty of keys, but the locks have stopped turning, so glucose still cannot get in.

    Who gets it: risk factors include family history, body weight, low physical activity, and certain genetic backgrounds. It used to be called adult-onset because it typically appeared after 40, but cases in young people have climbed sharply.

    Management: usually starts with changes to eating patterns, activity, and sometimes weight. Many people also take oral medication, and some eventually take insulin too.

    What they share: both push blood sugar above the normal range, both produce the same set of symptoms, both can be diagnosed with an A1C of 6.5% or higher, and neither has a cure yet — only management, though research is moving quickly.

    05

    Why the symptoms happen

    Every classic symptom traces back to one thing: glucose stuck in the blood instead of inside cells. Once you see the three mechanisms, you can reason out any symptom on the list.

    Read first

    Mechanism one: the body flushes the excess

    When glucose builds up past what the blood can carry, the kidneys start dumping the extra into urine to get rid of it. Urine is mostly water, so getting rid of all that glucose means losing a lot of fluid — and a body running low on fluid gets dehydrated. Dehydration is what drives several symptoms at once.

    Mechanism two: the cells are starving

    Meanwhile, the cells never got their fuel. They signal that they need energy, and the body responds the way it always does when it thinks it is short on food. When glucose stays out of reach, the body turns to its backup fuel and starts breaking down fat instead.

    Mechanism three: high glucose damages things directly

    Sugar sitting in the bloodstream at high levels causes problems on its own. It pulls fluid out of tissues that need it, it interferes with the white blood cells that fight infection, and bacteria grow more easily in a high-sugar environment.

    The symptoms below are the ones people notice first: frequent urination, constant thirst and dry mouth, hunger even right after eating, unexplained weight loss, fatigue and weakness, blurry vision, and cuts that heal slowly. Every one of them comes from one of those three mechanisms. See if you can work out which.

    06

    What untreated high blood sugar costs

    The symptoms are uncomfortable. The long-term damage is what management is actually trying to prevent.

    Read first

    Blood reaches every organ in your body, which means high blood sugar reaches every organ too. Over years, it damages the small blood vessels and nerves wherever blood goes. That damage builds quietly, often without symptoms, until it is significant — which is why people manage diabetes carefully long before they feel bad.

    The encouraging side: keeping glucose in range protects all of these at once, and most of these complications can be prevented, delayed, or treated when caught early. Tap each highlighted area below to see what is at stake.

    08

    Living with it: three situations

    Management is not one thing. It is a few tools working together, adjusted constantly based on what the numbers say.

    Read first

    The three-part toolkit

    Food. What you eat controls how much glucose enters the bloodstream and how fast. A balanced diet with portion control, getting carbohydrates from vegetables, whole grains, fruits, and legumes, is the foundation. There are no forbidden foods and no special "diabetic" products required — the recommendations look a lot like what is recommended for everyone.

    Movement. Physical activity makes cells more receptive to insulin and burns glucose directly. The general guideline is at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-to-vigorous activity.

    Medication. For Type 1, insulin is not optional. For Type 2, oral medications or newer drugs may be added, and some people use insulin as well.

    Monitoring: you cannot manage what you cannot measure

    A finger-stick meter gives a snapshot of blood glucose right now; a common target before meals is roughly 80 to 130 mg/dL. A continuous glucose monitor is a sensor worn on the body that tracks glucose day and night and shows trends rather than single points. The A1C test, done by a doctor, gives a three-month average. Ketone testing matters when blood sugar is very high or during illness.

    Two emergencies worth recognizing

    High blood sugar (hyperglycemia) comes on slowly: fatigue, extreme thirst, frequent urination. If there is not enough insulin, the body burns fat for fuel and produces ketones, which are toxic as they accumulate. That leads to diabetic ketoacidosis, or DKA, which requires medical treatment.

    Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) is the opposite and comes on fast — below about 70 mg/dL. Signs include shakiness, sweating, irritability or sudden mood changes, hunger, paleness, confusion, and loss of coordination. A person having a low can look and act like they are drunk, which is one reason it gets misread. The fix is fast-acting sugar: juice, glucose tablets, or hard candy.

    If someone is unconscious or cannot swallow, call 911. Never put food or drink in the mouth of a person who is unresponsive. Emergency responders and trained adults can give an injection of glucagon, a hormone that raises blood sugar quickly.
